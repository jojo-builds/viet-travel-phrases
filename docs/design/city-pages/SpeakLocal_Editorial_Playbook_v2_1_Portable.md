# SpeakLocal Editorial Playbook v2.1 Portable

## Purpose

This playbook is designed for a fresh ChatGPT, Codex, or agent session.

It should be used together with the **21 SpeakLocal example entries**. The examples are not just samples of tone. They are training anchors. A new session should study how the best examples make a traveler feel more confident, more oriented, and more ready to use a place.

SpeakLocal copy is not travel-blog copy. It is not SEO destination copy. It is not a restaurant review. It is not a generic phrasebook.

SpeakLocal copy is:

> **Evidence-backed traveler briefing.**

It answers:

> “What should I know before I go, what should I do first, and what can I say there?”

The user should finish a listing thinking:

> “Now I know how to use this place.”

Not merely:

> “Now I know what this place is.”

---

## What changed in v2.1

v2.1 keeps the v2 editorial core, then adds the lessons from the 21-example test.

The additions are:

1. A **canonical example anchor map** so new sessions know which examples to study first.
2. A stricter **scoring rubric** so 30/30 is rare.
3. A **verification taxonomy** with light, same-week, and status-blocking flags.
4. A **scarce-evidence fallback** for small markets, working streets, and thin-source listings.
5. A **Vietnamese phrase and place-name QA pass**.
6. A **neighborhood cluster pattern** for places like Japan Town Saigon.
7. A **new or recently renovated venue pattern** for places like Da Nang Museum.
8. A stronger **still-worth-it rule** so honesty does not become deflation.
9. A recommended **writer → grader → phrase QA → freshness QA** workflow.

# 1. Core product definition

SpeakLocal is **travel confidence infrastructure**.

The user is usually:

- English-first or U.S.-based.
- Already planning, imagining, or close to taking a trip.
- Excited, but slightly uncertain.
- Not trying to study the language deeply.
- Trying to feel locally aware before arrival.
- Looking for small, usable language wins.
- Sensitive to generic AI travel copy.

A listing should help them:

- Understand why the place matters.
- Picture what being there feels like.
- Make one smarter decision.
- Avoid an awkward first move.
- Avoid a disappointing expectation.
- Learn a few phrases they can actually use there.

The best SpeakLocal copy is:

- Specific.
- Calm.
- Useful.
- Lightly emotional.
- Honest without being cynical.
- Warm without being promotional.

---

# 2. The highest-level rule

## The first heading must be a traveler-use cue

The first heading should tell the traveler how to use the place before they read the body.

Good examples:

- **Confirm The Set First**
- **Choose Dry Or Close**
- **Walk Once Before Sitting**
- **Pick Three Anchors**
- **Check Weather Before Committing**
- **Go When The Tide Helps**
- **Know You Are Choosing A Theme Park**
- **Start Hot And Classic At The Source**
- **Choose A Destination, Not A Cruise**
- **Go For Snacks, Not Souvenirs**

Weak examples:

- **The Vibe**
- **Why This Works**
- **A Hidden Gem**
- **A Local Favorite**
- **A Vibrant Experience**
- **A Place To Remember**
- **An Authentic Stop**
- **A Must-Visit Landmark**

A good heading is not decorative. It carries decision value.

---

# 3. The voice rule

SpeakLocal sounds like:

- A calm, experienced traveler.
- A practical friend with good taste.
- Someone who notices physical details.
- Someone who understands traveler anxiety.
- Someone who wants the user to enjoy the place.
- Someone who can be honest without killing anticipation.

SpeakLocal does not sound like:

- A tourism board.
- A generic city guide.
- A restaurant review site.
- A Wikipedia entry.
- A luxury lifestyle magazine.
- A travel influencer caption.
- A chatbot trying to sound poetic.

## The emotional balance

Each entry should usually contain:

1. **One grounding truth** — what the place really is.
2. **One emotional reason to care** — why it can still feel memorable.
3. **One practical move** — what the traveler should do first.

Example:

> Ba Na Hills is a mountain theme park, not just a quiet Golden Bridge viewpoint. But when the sky opens, the cable-car climb from Da Nang heat into cooler mountain air can still feel cinematic. Go early, do the bridge first, then decide how much of the park you actually want.

That is the tone: real, useful, and still worth doing.

---

# 4. The main failure mode: replaceable copy

The biggest problem is not “bad writing.” It is **replaceable writing**.

Fail the copy if it could fit another place by swapping the name.

Bad:

> This vibrant market offers a wide range of local foods, souvenirs, and cultural experiences.

Better:

> Hàn Market gives you a quick read on central Da Nang: jars of mắm, stacks of dried squid, fabric stalls, rattan bags, and breakfast bowls all under one roof.

The second version has evidence density. It could not easily belong to another market.

Replaceable copy usually contains:

- Generic adjectives.
- Category repetition.
- Broad praise.
- No clear decision.
- No physical texture.
- No timing cue.
- No traveler action.

# 5. Use examples as training anchors, not templates

The examples teach behavior. They are not rigid templates.

A new session should study these examples first, because each one demonstrates a different SpeakLocal pattern.

## Canonical example anchors to study first

### 1. Bùi Viện Walking Street

Study this for **nightlife safety without fear-mongering**.

Best pattern:

> **Walk Once Before Sitting**

Why it works:

- Gives the traveler an immediate behavior.
- Explains the scene honestly.
- Keeps safety practical: phone, bag, prices, quick pitches.
- Avoids moralizing.
- Preserves curiosity.

Use this pattern for:

- Nightlife streets.
- Bar districts.
- Crowded entertainment areas.
- Places with touts, noise, or unclear pricing.

Key lesson:

> Tell the traveler how to look first, then choose.

---

### 2. Dragon Bridge

Study this for **simple landmarks with one real decision**.

Best pattern:

> **Choose Dry Or Close**

Why it works:

- Turns a bridge into a usable experience.
- Reduces the decision to close/spray vs. far/view.
- Includes schedule uncertainty.
- Pairs naturally with nearby riverfront stops.

Use this pattern for:

- Bridges.
- Light shows.
- Fire shows.
- Viewpoints.
- Short civic rituals.

Key lesson:

> Even a famous object needs a traveler-use decision.

---

### 3. Bale Well

Study this for **turning ambiguity into confidence**.

Best pattern:

> **Confirm The Set First**

Why it works:

- Keeps the hands-on meal exciting.
- Handles mixed reviews without sounding negative.
- Gives a concrete action before the meal starts.
- Makes phrases truly useful.

Use this pattern for:

- Set menus.
- Family-style restaurants.
- Tourist-famous local restaurants.
- Restaurants with unclear pricing.
- Places where staff demonstrate how to eat.

Key lesson:

> If a place has friction, convert that friction into a confidence cue.

Best pattern:

> **Pick Three Anchors**

Why it works:

- Stops the user from trying to read everything.

- Makes the museum feel human instead of academic.

Use this pattern for:

- Museums.
- Galleries.

> A museum listing should tell the traveler what to look at first.

### 8. The Note Coffee

Study this for **touristy but still enjoyable**.

Best pattern:

> **Order, Climb, Leave A Note**

Why it works:

- It does not pretend the place is undiscovered.

# 6. Evidence-first workflow

Do not ask a model to discover truth and write polished copy in the same pass.

Use two stages.

## Stage 1: Evidence extraction

Collect concrete details before writing.

Extract:

- Current status: open, closed, uncertain, needs verification.
- Location and role in the city.
- What the place is actually known for.
- Signature items, dishes, experiences, or features.
- Timing cues: morning, sunset, weekend, low tide, showtime, rush hour.
- Traveler risks: crowds, unclear pricing, heat, weather, closures, tourist traps.
- Review patterns, not single random reviews.
- Neighborhood context.
- Phrase opportunities tied to real tasks.

## Stage 2: Traveler briefing synthesis

Write from the evidence bundle only.

Do not invent:

- Hours.
- Prices.
- Signature dishes.
- Closure status.
- Schedules.
- Safety claims.
- Awards.
- Neighborhood claims.

If evidence conflicts, flag it instead of smoothing it away.

---

# 7. Source and verification rules

Use source classes in this order:

1. Official venue source.
2. Official tourism or government source.
3. Current operational source.
4. Reputable editorial source.
5. Recent review-pattern source.

Source notes are internal. They help editors, Codex, QA, and regeneration. They should not appear inside the normal app experience.

The user-facing app should feel effortlessly informed, not researched.

---

# 8. Verification taxonomy

Not all verification issues are equal.

## Light verification

These can be checked before publication, but they usually do not block drafting.

Examples:

- Standard opening hours.
- Standard ticket prices.
- Address formatting.
- Basic menu changes.
- Branch contact details.
- Current English interpretation availability.

Copy rule:

> Write normal copy, but flag the item for pre-publication check.

## Same-week verification

These should be checked close to the travel date or publication date.

Examples:

- Fire or water shows.
- Weather-dependent attractions.
- Tides.
- Cable-car operations.
- Boat routes.
- Hiking trails.
- Nightlife pedestrianization.
- Market stall availability.
- Pop-up exhibits.
- Transport windows.
- Festival timing.

Copy rule:

> Write normal copy only if the listing does not depend on a precise claim. Add language like “recheck the same week” when the user’s plan depends on it.

## Status-blocking verification

These block normal publication.

Examples:

- Possible closure.
- Conflicting official sources.
- Reopened after food-safety closure.
- Relocated branch with unclear address.
- New venue with unclear entrance/status.
- Venue not listed on current official location page.

Copy rule:

> Do not publish normal app copy until resolved. Return `needs_verification` with a clear issue.

Suggested status object:

```json
{
  "status": "needs_verification",
  "verification_level": "status_blocking",
  "status_issue": "Official sources conflict on whether this branch is currently operating."
}
```

# 9. Scarce-evidence fallback

Some real app listings will not have rich official sources.

This is common for:

- Small working markets.
- Neighborhood food stalls.
- Local snack areas.
- Streets without official tourism pages.
- Minor beaches or viewpoints.
- Small cafés without a website.

Do not inflate thin evidence.

When evidence is sparse:

- Make the listing shorter.
- Use fewer claims.
- Anchor the listing around one behavior.
- Avoid exact vendor names unless verified.
- Avoid fixed prices.
- Avoid precise hours.
- Avoid saying “famous” unless strongly supported.
- Use phrases for pointing, small portions, spice, cash, or directions.
- Add a verification flag.

Good scarce-evidence copy:

> **Go For Snacks, Not Souvenirs**  
> Bắc Mỹ An Market is best as a small snack stop near the Mỹ An side of Da Nang. Start with avocado ice cream, then ask about a hot snack only if you are still hungry. Bring small cash, point politely, check spice, and keep portions small.

Bad scarce-evidence copy:

> Bắc Mỹ An Market is one of Da Nang’s most famous food destinations, offering countless authentic dishes and unforgettable local experiences.

The good version is narrower, safer, and more useful.

# 10. Category-specific editorial jobs

## Markets

The job is to reduce sensory overwhelm.

Focus on:

- What to do first.
- Whether it is better for food, gifts, produce, or orientation.
- What to eat first.
- When to go.
- Cash, bargaining, heat, crowd, and pointing cues.

Strong examples to study:

- Hàn Market.
- Cồn Market.
- Bắc Mỹ An Market.

Good heading patterns:

- **A Market For First Bearings**
- **The Food-First Market**
- **Go For Snacks, Not Souvenirs**
- **Start With A Bowl**
- **Morning For Food, Later For Gifts**

Avoid:

- “A shopper’s paradise.”
- “A bustling market full of local flavor.”

---

## Restaurants and food counters

The job is to teach ordering confidence.

Focus on:

- What to order first.
- How the service works.
- Whether to confirm price, spice, set menu, shellfish, or portion size.
- Whether the place is fast, slow, chaotic, formal, casual, touristy, local, or ritual-based.

Strong examples to study:

- Bale Well.
- Bánh Mì Phượng.
- Madam Khanh.
- White Rose Restaurant.

Good heading patterns:

- **Confirm The Set First**
- **Decide Before The Counter**
- **Choose Your Heat Before Ordering**
- **Start With The Dumpling Source**
- **Go Classic First**

Avoid:

- “Delicious local cuisine.”
- “A must-try restaurant.”

---

## Cafés

The job is to explain the ritual.

Focus on:

- What to order first.
- The seating or ordering flow.
- Whether it is good for lingering, photos, history, quiet, view, or novelty.
- How sweet, strong, crowded, or intense the drink is.

Strong examples to study:

- Cà phê Giảng.
- The Note Coffee.

Good heading patterns:

- **Start Hot And Classic At The Source**
- **Small Cups, Close Tables**
- **Order, Climb, Leave A Note**
- **Egg Coffee, Then A Note**

Avoid:

- “Cozy café vibes.”
- “Perfect for coffee lovers.”

## Streets and historic walks

The job is to build a mental map.

Focus on:

- Where to start.
- Which direction to walk.
- What anchors matter.
- What this street does and does not represent.
- Whether it is best for food, nightlife, history, shopping, temples, or orientation.

Strong example to study:

- Đồng Khởi Street.

Good heading patterns:

- **Walk The Spine Toward The River**
- **Use Landmarks As Steps**
- **Early Before It Thickens**

Avoid:

- “Explore the vibrant streets.”
- “A charming neighborhood.”

## Neighborhoods and clusters

The job is to help the traveler enter the area without feeling lost.

Focus on:

- First anchor.
- First street or alley.
- What changes from day to night.
- What not to enter blindly.
- Whether it is a restaurant cluster, nightlife area, market district, temple district, or shopping area.

Strong examples to study:

- Chợ Lớn.
- Japan Town Saigon.
- Bùi Viện Walking Street.

Good heading patterns:

- **Anchor The District First**
- **Choose An Alley Before Dinner**
- **Walk Once Before Sitting**

Avoid:

- “A vibrant neighborhood.”
- “A charming cultural quarter.”

# 11. Neighborhood cluster pattern

Use this for areas that are not a single attraction.

Examples:

- Japan Town Saigon.
- Chợ Lớn.
- Bùi Viện.
- Night market districts.
- Dining alleys.
- Bar clusters.
- Shopping streets.

Required elements:

1. Name the first anchor.
2. Name the first street, alley, or entrance point.
3. Explain the area’s job.
4. Explain when it changes character.
5. Warn gently if door/business types vary.
6. Give one first action.

Example:

> **Choose An Alley Before Dinner**  
> Start around Lê Thánh Tôn and Thái Văn Lung, choose one alley, then decide: ramen, izakaya food, a quiet drink, or just a look. Read the door before entering because restaurants, bars, and nightlife spots sit close together. Early dinner is easier than late-night wandering.

Tone note:

Use practical language, not moralizing language.

Better:

> Read the door before entering; restaurants, bars, and nightlife spots sit close together here.

Riskier:

> Be careful because adult-oriented businesses are everywhere.

The first version gives useful guidance without making the user feel anxious.

---

# 12. New or recently renovated venue pattern

Use this for new museums, reopened venues, renovated branches, relocated cafés, and places where entry flow may have changed.

Required elements:

1. Explain why the place matters now.
2. Give the traveler one first anchor.
3. Avoid overclaiming current exhibits, hours, or entrance rules.
4. Flag entrance, ticket, hours, and current access for verification.
5. Keep the user-facing copy simple.

Example:

> **Start With The City, Not The Beach**  
> Use Da Nang Museum when you want the city behind the bridges and beaches. Start with one floor theme — city history, wartime context, or regional culture — instead of reading every display. Confirm the entrance, hours, and ticket price before going, then pair it with a Hàn River walk.

If the venue’s current status is unclear, do not write normal copy. Return `needs_verification`.

# 14. Mobile display version

The full entry is for training and QA.

The actual app should usually show a compressed version first.

## Mobile first-screen target

Aim for:

1. One traveler-use heading.
2. A two-sentence briefing.
3. One practical do-first cue.
4. Two to four useful phrases.

## Mobile word target

For most listings:

- **Compressed app version:** 55-110 words before phrases.
- **Expanded internal version:** 150-240 words before phrases.
- **Source notes:** internal only.

## Compression rule

The mobile version should keep:

- The first decision.
- One physical detail.
- One timing or expectation cue.
- One useful action.

It should cut:

- Extra background.
- Duplicate mood.
- Long setup.
- Any source-like language.

Example:

> **Choose Dry Or Close**  
> Stand near Dragon Bridge if you want to feel the spray; stay on the riverbank if you want the clean view. On show nights, arrive early enough to choose your side, then recheck the schedule that week. Pair it with the Hàn River walk or Sơn Trà Night Market.

# 15. Phrase policy

The phrases should be tied to real actions at that listing.

Good phrase tasks:

- Asking for a menu.
- Ordering the signature item.
- Asking for less sugar.
- Asking for no chili.
- Confirming a set price.
- Asking which way to a landmark.
- Asking if photos are okay.
- Asking when a show starts.
- Asking whether a spot gets wet.
- Asking about tide, fog, last cable car, return trip, or life jackets.
- Calling a taxi or returning to the hotel.

Bad phrase tasks:

- Generic travel phrases unrelated to the place.
- Airport phrases on a café page.
- Hotel phrases on a market page.
- Overly complex conversation scripts.
- Phrases the traveler is unlikely to actually say.

Use:

- 2-4 phrases for mobile.
- 3-5 phrases for expanded examples.

Each phrase should include Vietnamese and English meaning.

# 16. Vietnamese phrase and place-name QA

Every batch needs a separate Vietnamese QA pass.

Check:

- Diacritics.
- Naturalness.
- Place-name order.
- Whether locals would actually say it that way.
- Whether the English translation matches.
- Whether the phrase is too formal, too awkward, or too long.
- Whether a foreign traveler could realistically say it.

Watch for place-name reversals.

Example issue:

- **Hải Vọng Đài** should not become **Vọng Hải Đài**.

Better phrase:

> **"Đường lên Hải Vọng Đài ở đâu?"** - Where is the way to Hải Vọng Đài?

Also check common typo risk:

- **ca cao trứng** = egg cocoa.
- **cà phê trứng** = egg coffee.
- **mì Quảng** uses correct diacritics.
- **bánh bao bánh vạc** is the white rose dumpling name.

If unsure, flag for native-speaker review rather than inventing confidence.

# 17. Banned and risky language

Ban these unless quoting a source:

- hidden gem
- must-visit
- vibrant
- bustling
- nestled
- offers something for everyone
- rich culture
- paradise
- unforgettable experience
- authentic experience
- charming
- iconic landmark
- local favorite
- perfect spot
- culinary gem
- where tradition meets modernity
- a feast for the senses
- bucket-list
- Instagrammable
- magical
- enchanting

Also avoid:

- Empty mood lines.
- Fake enthusiasm.
- Corporate tourism-board phrasing.
- Overexplaining obvious facts.
- “This place is known for...” without saying how the traveler should use it.
- Repeating the category as the insight.

Bad:

> This café is known for coffee and a cozy atmosphere.

Better:

> Come for one hot egg coffee, stir slowly, and treat the tiny cup more like dessert than a normal caffeine stop.

# 18. The still-worth-it rule

A listing can be honest, but it should not become deflationary.

Every entry should include one sentence that explains why the place is still worth doing.

This sentence should be grounded, not hypey.

Good:

> When the sky opens, the cable-car climb from Da Nang heat into mountain air can still feel cinematic.

Good:

> The best part is not the photo alone; it is watching incense paste, bamboo, color, and scent become one object by hand.

Good:

> It is touristy, but the ritual is simple enough to enjoy: order, climb, read, leave a note.

Bad:

> This unforgettable attraction will leave you with memories for a lifetime.

Bad:

> It is touristy and probably not worth it.

SpeakLocal should say:

> “Here is the real version, and here is why it can still be worth doing.”

# 19. Stricter scoring rubric

Score each listing from 0-5 in each category.

## 1. Place specificity

5 = Concrete details that could only belong here.  
3 = Some real details, but partly generic.  
1 = Could fit many places.

## 2. Traveler decision value

5 = Teaches what to do, when to go, what to order, or what to avoid.  
3 = Useful but mostly descriptive.  
1 = No clear decision help.

## 3. Evidence discipline

5 = All claims are sourced or from supplied evidence; uncertainty is flagged.  
3 = Mostly grounded, but a few claims need verification.  
1 = Invents or overstates.

## 4. Mobile readability

5 = Short, scannable, first-screen friendly.  
3 = Strong copy but too long for mobile.  
1 = Dense, essay-like, or repetitive.

## 5. Phrase relevance

5 = Phrases match real tasks at this place.  
3 = Phrases are useful but somewhat generic.  
1 = Phrasebook drift.

## 6. Tone balance

5 = Honest, warm, and still makes the place feel worth doing.  
3 = Slightly too promotional or slightly too skeptical.  
1 = Hypey, cynical, or flat.

## v2.1 score meanings

**30/30**  
Publish-quality after factual verification. No editorial rewrite needed. Rare.

**28-29**  
Gold-standard quality. Minor QA or verification may remain.

**24-27**  
Strong draft. Needs more evidence, compression, or specificity.

**21-23**  
Usable direction. Requires revision before publication.

**20 or below**  
Reject and regenerate.

## Score caps

Use these caps to prevent self-scoring inflation.

- If the writer is scoring their own work, **30/30 should be rare**.
- If any same-week verification flag remains, score should usually be **29 or below**.
- If evidence is sparse, score should usually be **27 or below**.
- If a listing has status-blocking uncertainty, do not score normal copy. Return `needs_verification`.
- If Vietnamese phrase QA has not been completed, score should usually be **29 or below**.
- If the copy is strong but too long for mobile, score should usually be **27 or below**.

The same session that writes the copy should not be treated as the final grader.

# 20. Required QA tests

## 1. Replaceability test

Remove the place name.

Could this copy fit another café, market, bridge, or neighborhood?

If yes, fail.

## 2. Decision test

Does the copy help the traveler make a decision?

Examples:

- What to order first.
- Where to start walking.
- When to go.
- Whether to confirm price.
- Whether to stand close or far.
- Whether this is a standalone destination or route stop.

If no, revise.

## 3. Phrase task test

Would the user realistically say these phrases at this place?

If no, replace them.

## 4. Anti-hype test

Does the copy overpromise?

If yes, ground it with a real expectation.

## 5. Anti-cynicism test

Does the copy become so practical that it kills excitement?

If yes, add one honest emotional reason to care.

## 6. Verification-level test

Is the issue light, same-week, or status-blocking?

If status-blocking, stop normal copy.

## 7. Vietnamese QA test

Are names, diacritics, grammar, and translations correct enough for publication?

If not, flag for native-speaker review.

# 21. Recommended production workflow

Do not rely on one model pass.

Use four roles.

## 1. Evidence extractor

Tasks:

- Gather source notes.
- Identify source conflicts.
- Mark verification level.
- Extract traveler-use facts.

Output:

- Evidence bundle.
- Verification flags.
- Phrase opportunities.

## 2. Writer

Tasks:

- Write expanded entry.
- Write mobile entry.
- Add useful phrases.
- Do not invent facts.

Output:

- Draft listing.

## 3. Editorial grader

Tasks:

- Apply the scoring rubric.
- Run QA tests.
- Reject or request revision.
- Keep score strict.

Output:

- Score and required edits.

## 4. Vietnamese / freshness QA

Tasks:

- Check Vietnamese phrases.
- Check place-name order.
- Check hours, status, tickets, schedules, menus, and current access.
- Mark publication readiness.

Output:

- Pass / revise / needs verification.

If possible, do not let the same session perform all roles without a separate review pass.

# 22. Editing existing 21 examples

When editing the 21 example entries, do not rewrite from scratch unless necessary.

Use this order:

1. Preserve strong traveler-use headings.
2. Remove or soften headings that are too poetic or abstract.
3. Add one physical detail where the entry feels intellectual.
4. Compress long bodies for mobile.
5. Keep honest cautions, but add one reason the place is still worth doing.
6. Remove source notes from app-facing copy.
7. Keep source notes internal.
8. Run Vietnamese phrase QA.
9. Re-score using the stricter v2.1 rubric.

# 26. Final standard

A finished SpeakLocal listing should make the traveler think:

> “Now I know how to use this place.”

It should not merely make them think:

> “Now I know what this place is.”

That is the difference between content and traveler intelligence.
